01

Accounting equation and the balance sheet idea
Plain-English rule

Assets are resources the business controls. Liabilities are what it owes to outsiders. Equity is the residual interest of the owners. The equation Assets = Liabilities + Equity must hold after every single transaction.

Expanded version

Assets = Liabilities + Capital + Income − Expenses. Profit increases equity. Drawings and dividends reduce it. This is why the statement of profit or loss connects to the statement of financial position.

Worked example

Owner contributes ₦1,000,000 cash: assets rise ₦1,000,000 and equity rises ₦1,000,000. The business buys equipment for ₦400,000 cash: cash falls ₦400,000 and equipment rises ₦400,000. Assets stay at ₦1,000,000.

Common trap

Profit is not cash. A credit sale raises profit and receivables without a naira entering the bank. A profitable business can still fail from poor cash timing.

Recall prompts
  1. Owner invests cash. Which two elements of the equation move, and in which direction?
  2. A company buys equipment on credit. What happens to assets and liabilities?
  3. Why can a profitable company run out of cash?
Show model answers
  1. Assets increase (cash) and equity increases (capital), both by the same amount.
  2. Assets increase (equipment) and liabilities increase (payables), both by the same amount.
  3. Profit is an accrual measure. Credit sales, slow collections, inventory build-up, or heavy cash expenses can leave a profitable business short of cash.
Technology Risk link

System interfaces and automated postings must preserve completeness and accuracy. A ledger can look perfectly balanced while a failed interface silently omits part of the population. That is exactly the kind of gap population testing catches.

02

Accounting defined: purpose, users, and types of business
Plain-English rule

Accounting is the process of identifying, recording, classifying, summarising, and communicating economic information about an entity so that users can make informed decisions. It is called the language of business for a reason: it turns activity into numbers people can act on.

Why you learn it
  • To read and interpret financial statements instead of nodding politely at them.
  • To see how business activity becomes reported numbers, and where it can go wrong.
  • To understand what finance teams own, so your Technology Risk questions land in their language.
  • To spot fraud, error, and misstatement risk in systems and processes.
Main types of business
  • Sole proprietorship: one owner, simple to set up, owner has unlimited liability, business is not a separate legal person. Equity shows as a capital account.
  • Partnership: two or more owners sharing capital and profits, generally with unlimited liability in a general partnership. A limited liability partnership limits members' liability.
  • Limited liability company: a separate legal person owned by shareholders whose liability is limited to their shares. Registered under CAMA in Nigeria. Private (Ltd) or public (Plc). Equity shows as share capital plus retained earnings.
Common trap

Do not mix up the owner and the business. In accounting, the business is treated as separate from its owner, which is why drawings and owner contributions are recorded rather than ignored.

Two statements to name in any answer

The balance sheet (statement of financial position) shows assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time. The income statement (profit or loss) shows revenue, expenses, and profit over a period. One is a photograph, the other is a video.

Recall prompts
  1. Define accounting in one sentence without using the word money.
  2. Name the three main types of business and one liability feature of each.
  3. Which statement is a photograph and which is a video?
Show model answers
  1. Accounting identifies, records, summarises, and communicates economic information so users can make informed decisions.
  2. Sole proprietorship: unlimited liability. General partnership: unlimited liability. Limited liability company: liability limited to the shares held.
  3. The statement of financial position is the photograph at a point in time. The income statement is the video covering a period.
Technology Risk link

Every business type still produces financial statements, and every financial statement is fed by systems. Your edge is understanding both the business structure and the technology chain behind the numbers.

03

Debits, credits, and the DEALER rule
Plain-English rule

Debit means the left side of an account. Credit means the right side. DEALER: Drawings and dividends, Expenses, Assets increase with a debit. Liabilities, Equity, Revenue increase with a credit. Every transaction has at least one debit and one credit, and they must be equal.

Worked example

Pay ₦80,000 rent in cash: debit Rent Expense ₦80,000, credit Cash ₦80,000. Make a ₦50,000 cash sale: debit Cash ₦50,000, credit Sales Revenue ₦50,000.

Common trap

Debit does not mean good and credit does not mean bad. They are sides of an account, not moral verdicts. Also remember that for a bank, customer deposits are liabilities, because the bank owes that money back.

Recall prompts
  1. Which side increases an asset? Which side increases revenue?
  2. Record a ₦50,000 cash sale without looking.
  3. Why are customer deposits a liability on a bank's balance sheet?
Show model answers
  1. Debits increase assets. Credits increase revenue.
  2. Dr Cash ₦50,000 / Cr Sales revenue ₦50,000.
  3. Because the bank owes that money back to the depositor. It controls the cash but carries an obligation to repay.
Technology Risk link

Application controls often enforce balanced journal entries at the point of posting. Your audit question is whether the system actually prevents or flags invalid postings, and whether the override path is logged.

04

Double entry and the journal
Plain-English rule

Double entry is an accounting system that records a transaction in a minimum of two accounts. It is based on a dual aspect: debit and credit. The principle: every debit must have an equal and corresponding credit. The journal records the date, accounts, amounts, narration, and reference. Posting moves those entries into ledger accounts, creating traceability from a source document to the financial statements.

Worked example

Buy inventory on credit for ₦250,000: debit Inventory ₦250,000, credit Accounts Payable ₦250,000. Pay the supplier later: debit Accounts Payable ₦250,000, credit Bank ₦250,000.

Follow-up entries: credit sale and settlement
  1. Sale on credit: Dr Receivables / Cr Sales revenue.
  2. Customer pays later: Dr Cash / Cr Receivables.
  3. No new revenue is recorded when the customer pays. Revenue was recognised at the sale. The follow-up entry only converts a receivable into cash.
The two entries behind every sale (perpetual inventory)

Selling goods for ₦1,000,000 that cost ₦400,000 takes two entries. Revenue side: Dr Receivables or Cash ₦1,000,000 / Cr Sales ₦1,000,000. Cost side: Dr Cost of sales ₦400,000 / Cr Inventory ₦400,000. Gross profit is ₦600,000. In a periodic system, the cost side shows up through purchases and the closing inventory adjustment instead of a cost of sales account.

Practice entries from the slides
  • Pay rent ₦200 in cash: Dr Rent expense ₦200 / Cr Cash ₦200.
  • Pay motor expenses ₦55 by cheque: Dr Motor expenses ₦55 / Cr Bank ₦55.
  • Receive ₦24 cash as interest income: Dr Cash ₦24 / Cr Interest income ₦24.
Permanent and temporary accounts

Asset, liability, and equity ledgers are permanent accounts: their balances are carried down (balance c/d) and brought forward into the next accounting period, then reported in the statement of financial position. Revenue and expense accounts are temporary (nominal) accounts: at period end they are closed to the income summary or profit and loss account, and their balances become zero.

Common trap

A balanced entry can still be wrong. It may hit the wrong account, wrong period, wrong entity, or wrong cost centre. Balance proves arithmetic, not truth.

Recall prompts
  1. What is the entry for inventory bought on credit?
  2. Name two ways a balanced journal can still be wrong.
  3. Why is journal approval a control rather than a formality?
Show model answers
  1. Dr Inventory (or Purchases) / Cr Accounts payable.
  2. Wrong account, wrong period, wrong entity, wrong cost centre, or a completely omitted entry masked by a compensating error.
  3. It puts a second person between the preparer and the ledger, catching error and reducing fraud risk. The approval is also audit evidence.
Technology Risk link

For an ERP or core banking client, ask who can post, who can approve, what is automated, and whether the audit trail survives. Manual journal entry access is one of the most abused doors in any finance system.

05

T-accounts, normal balances, and closing entries
Plain-English rule

A T-account is the visual form of a ledger account. Debits sit on the left, credits sit on the right. At period end, the difference is the balance: carried down (c/d) on the smaller side and brought down (b/d) on the larger side to start the next period.

Normal balances
  • Debit-balanced accounts: assets, expenses, drawings, dividends, purchases.
  • Credit-balanced accounts: liabilities, equity or capital, revenue or sales, accruals.
  • Contra accounts reverse the pattern: accumulated depreciation is credit-balanced, drawings is debit-balanced.
Closing entries for a sole trader
  1. Close revenue to the profit and loss account: debit sales, credit P&L.
  2. Close expenses and purchases to the P&L account: debit P&L, credit each expense account.
  3. The P&L balance is the profit or loss. Transfer it to capital: profit means debit P&L, credit capital. A loss reverses.
  4. Assets and liabilities are never closed. Their balances are carried forward to the statement of financial position.
Worked mini example

Sales of ₦400,000, purchases of ₦250,000, rent of ₦60,000. Close sales: Dr Sales 400,000 / Cr P&L 400,000. Close purchases and rent: Dr P&L 310,000 / Cr Purchases 250,000 / Cr Rent 60,000. The P&L shows a ₦90,000 profit, transferred to capital: Dr P&L 90,000 / Cr Capital 90,000.

Common trap

Only temporary (nominal) accounts close: revenue, expenses, purchases, and the P&L itself. Permanent (real) accounts such as cash, equipment, loans, and payables stay open and carry forward. Closing a real account destroys the balance sheet.

Recall prompts
  1. Which accounts have a normal debit balance?
  2. Write the four closing steps for a sole trader in order.
  3. Why do asset and liability balances appear on the statement of financial position after closing?
Show model answers
  1. Assets, expenses, drawings and purchases.
  2. Close revenue to the P&L, close expenses and purchases to the P&L, transfer the profit or loss to capital, then leave asset and liability balances open.
  3. They are permanent accounts. Their balances continue into the next period and are presented in the statement of financial position.
Technology Risk link

Closing and carry-forward are automated in every ERP and core banking system. When period-end routines change in a system upgrade, the closing logic is a financial reporting control that deserves a controlled change record.

06

The accounting cycle and the trial balance
Plain-English rule

The cycle: source documents, journal, post to ledger, extract the trial balance, make adjusting entries, produce the adjusted trial balance, prepare the financial statements, then close the temporary accounts. A trial balance simply lists closing balances and proves total debits equal total credits.

Worked example

Total debits of ₦4,200,000 against total credits of ₦4,200,000: the trial balance agrees. It still tells you nothing about whether a whole transaction was omitted or an amount was posted to the wrong account.

Common trap

A balanced trial balance is not proof of accuracy. Errors of omission (no entry at all), errors of commission (wrong account), and compensating errors all survive a balanced trial balance.

Recall prompts
  1. List the steps of the accounting cycle in order.
  2. What exactly does a trial balance prove, and what does it not prove?
  3. Where do adjusting entries fit in the cycle?
Show model answers
  1. Source documents, journal, ledger, trial balance, adjusting entries, adjusted trial balance, financial statements, closing entries.
  2. It proves total debits equal total credits. It does not prove completeness, correct classification, authorisation, or correct period.
  3. After the initial trial balance and before the adjusted trial balance and the financial statements.
Technology Risk link

Automated trial balances depend on interfaces, posting rules, and period controls. When a system upgrade changes posting logic, the trial balance can still balance while the meaning of a balance changes. That is why change management is a financial reporting control.

07

Accruals, prepayments, depreciation, and other adjustments
Plain-English rule

The accrual basis matches income and expenses to the period they belong to, not the period cash moves. Accruals record expenses or income before cash moves. Prepayments record cash before the benefit is consumed. Depreciation allocates the cost of a long-lived asset across its useful life.

Worked examples
  • Unpaid December rent of ₦100,000: debit Rent Expense, credit Accrued Expenses.
  • Prepaid insurance of ₦120,000 for twelve months: debit Prepaid Insurance, credit Cash, then release ₦10,000 monthly to expense.
  • Equipment of ₦1,200,000 over five years, straight line: ₦240,000 depreciation each year. Debit Depreciation Expense, credit Accumulated Depreciation.
  • Customer owes ₦30,000 and will not pay: debit Impairment Expense, credit Trade Receivables.
Common trap

Depreciation is not a cash payment. It is the allocation of a past cash cost. Accumulated depreciation is a contra-asset account and grows over the asset's life, while the asset stays at cost in the ledger.

Recall prompts
  1. Give the two entries for an accrued expense and for a prepayment.
  2. Compute straight-line depreciation on ₦1,200,000 over five years with no residual value.
  3. Why is accumulated depreciation not deducted from the asset account itself?
Show model answers
  1. Accrued expense: Dr Expense / Cr Accruals. Prepayment: Dr Prepaid expense / Cr Cash, then release to expense as the benefit is consumed.
  2. ₦1,200,000 ÷ 5 = ₦240,000 per year.
  3. So the asset stays at cost and total depreciation to date stays visible. Net book value is cost minus accumulated depreciation.
Technology Risk link

Adjustment calculations are often automated, which makes the underlying configuration, master data, and change history audit-relevant. If depreciation logic changes after a system patch, someone must have approved and tested that change.

08

The complete set of financial statements
Plain-English rule

Financial statements are a structured set of documents showing an entity's financial position, performance, and cash flows over a period or at a point in time. Their objective: give users reliable, decision-useful information prepared under the applicable framework and local law. In Nigeria the framework is typically IFRS (under the FRC framework), supplemented by CAMA and sectoral regulators.

The main statements (IAS 1 set)
  • Statement of Financial Position (SOFP): assets, liabilities, equity at a date.
  • Statement of Profit or Loss and Other Comprehensive Income (SOCI): performance for the period.
  • Statement of Changes in Equity (SOCE): movements in each component of equity.
  • Statement of Cash Flows (SOCF): actual cash movements.
  • Notes to the Accounts: policies, breakdowns, judgements, related-party transactions.
  • Plus comparative information for the prior period.
Reports in the annual financial statements
  • Directors' report: required by CAMA. Legal form, principal activities, principal risks, directors and their interests, charitable contributions, shareholding structure, material contracts, employee matters, events after the reporting date. Auditors review it for consistency with the accounts.
  • Chairman's report: optional, not required by CAMA 2020. A high-level narrative from the Board Chair; not audited and may contain subjective opinions.
  • Auditor's report: the independent opinion on whether the statements present fairly, in all material respects, under the applicable framework.
Auditor's opinion types (exam favourite)
  • Unmodified (clean): statements are free from material misstatement and comply with the framework. True and fair view.
  • Qualified: true and fair except for one specific issue. The problem is material but not pervasive.
  • Adverse: the auditors disagree with management and the misstatements are material and pervasive. The statements cannot be trusted as a whole.
  • Disclaimer: the auditors could not obtain sufficient evidence (for example, a scope limitation or destroyed records), so no opinion is expressed.
Users of the financial statements
  • Investors: profitability and growth potential before investing.
  • Creditors: creditworthiness and the risk of lending.
  • Management: performance analysis, strategy, and planning.
  • Government and regulators: compliance and determination of taxable income.
  • Employees: financial health, job security, and compensation.
  • Auditors: verifying accuracy and compliance with standards.
What each one answers
  • Financial position: what do we own and owe right now?
  • Profit or loss: how did we perform over the period?
  • Changes in equity: what moved owners' interest and why?
  • Cash flows: where did cash actually come from and go?
  • Notes: the detail and judgement behind the numbers.
Worked example

Classify: customer receipts are operating cash flow. Buying equipment is investing. Repaying a bank loan is financing. Depreciation appears in profit or loss but never as a cash payment, so it is adjusted for in the operating section of the cash flow statement.

Common trap

The statement of financial position is a photograph at one date. The statement of profit or loss is a video of the whole period. Mixing those two ideas is the fastest way to lose easy marks.

Recall prompts
  1. Name the five components of a complete set of financial statements.
  2. Classify loan repayment, equipment purchase, and customer receipts into cash flow categories.
  3. Why are the notes not optional?
Show model answers
  1. Statement of financial position, statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income, statement of changes in equity, statement of cash flows, and notes with accounting policies and comparatives.
  2. Loan repayment: financing. Equipment purchase: investing. Customer receipts: operating.
  3. They explain policies, estimates, judgements and material detail. Without them the statements are incomplete and hard to understand.
Technology Risk link

Learn which applications, interfaces, and end-of-day jobs feed each statement. Financial statement assurance is often technology assurance wearing a different jacket.

09

Financial statement analysis and ratios
Plain-English rule

Financial analysis is the process of evaluating businesses, projects, budgets, and other finance-related transactions to determine their performance and suitability. It is used to judge whether an entity is stable, solvent, liquid, or profitable enough to warrant investment. We analyse to assess past performance, assess the current position, predict profitability and growth, assess operational efficiency, and predict bankruptcy or failure.

The four analysis techniques
  • Horizontal analysis: comparison of two periods, using absolute changes and percentage changes.
  • Trend analysis: three or more periods, a continuation of horizontal analysis. The base year is the earliest year in the data set.
  • Vertical analysis: a single period. On the income statement, each line is expressed as a percentage of net sales.
  • Ratio analysis: relationships between financial statement totals, compared to budgets and industry benchmarks. Categories: liquidity, efficiency, leverage (capital structure), profitability, and solvency.
Early warning signals
  • Consistent increase in receivables: may signal weak credit and collection policies; uncollected debts risk bad debts and cash shortages.
  • High inventory levels: slow-moving sales, obsolete or damaged goods, and cash tied up in stock.
  • Decreasing profit margins: possible going concern issues from declining sales, poor expense management, or high debt servicing costs.
  • High debt capital: heavy borrowing, dangerous if interest rates rise; may lead to insolvency.
Core formulas (slide set)
  • Gross profit margin = gross profit ÷ sales (× 100)
  • Net profit margin = net profit ÷ sales (× 100)
  • Return on capital employed = profit before interest and tax ÷ capital employed
  • Earnings per share = net profit available to ordinary shareholders ÷ average number of ordinary shares
  • Return on equity = net profit ÷ average equity (× 100)
  • Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities
  • Quick (acid test) ratio = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities
  • Net working capital = current assets − current liabilities
  • Debtors turnover = sales ÷ average trade debtors; days = average debtors ÷ sales × calendar days
  • Stock turnover = cost of goods sold ÷ average stock
  • Gearing = long-term loans ÷ ordinary shareholders' funds (× 100)
  • Debt to equity = total debt ÷ total equity
  • Interest cover = EBIT ÷ finance costs
Worked example

Current assets ₦8,000,000, inventory ₦2,000,000, current liabilities ₦4,000,000. Current ratio is 2.0. Quick ratio is (8,000,000 − 2,000,000) ÷ 4,000,000 = 1.5. The business can cover short-term debts without selling inventory.

Common trap

A high current ratio can hide slow-moving inventory or receivables that will never be collected. Ratios are questions, not conclusions. High profit with weak operating cash flow is a classic quality-of-earnings warning.

Recall prompts
  1. Write the current ratio and quick ratio formulas.
  2. A company has strong profit but weak operating cash flow. Name two possible causes.
  3. Why does ROE matter more to owners than net margin alone?
Show model answers
  1. Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities. Quick ratio = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities.
  2. Slow collections, inventory build-up, aggressive revenue recognition, or rapid repayment of payables.
  3. ROE measures the return on the owners' capital, which is what shareholders actually earn. Net margin ignores how much capital was used to produce the profit.
Technology Risk link

Analytics work depends on clean data. Duplicate customers, orphaned invoices, and stale master data quietly distort every ratio. Population-level testing exists precisely because samples hide these patterns.

10

IFRS: the framework and the standards worth knowing
Plain-English rule

IFRS stands for International Financial Reporting Standards: globally recognised accounting standards used to prepare financial statements. They are developed and issued by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), whose objective is a common accounting language that can be applied across different countries.

IFRS is not one standard: it comprises
  • International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)
  • International Accounting Standards (IAS)
  • IFRIC Interpretations (International Financial Reporting Interpretations Committee)
  • Standards Interpretation Committee (SIC) Interpretations
  • The Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting
Nigeria's adoption

Nigerian companies historically used Statements of Accounting Standards (SAS) issued by the Nigerian Accounting Standards Board (NASB). Nigeria adopted IFRS through a phased implementation beginning in 2012. Benefits: improved transparency, increased foreign investment, enhanced credibility, easier comparison, and better access to global capital markets.

Changes to traditional concepts under IFRS
  • Prudence: the objective is neutrality rather than excessive conservatism. Recognise gains and losses when the accounting criteria are met.
  • Matching: no longer the primary driver. IFRS uses an asset-liability approach: does the item meet the definition of an asset or a liability?
  • Historical cost: IFRS moves toward fair value, measuring assets at current market value in the relevant cases.
  • Consistency: still important, but IFRS allows a change in accounting policy when required by a new standard, or when the change gives more reliable and relevant information.
SAS to IFRS: the name changes
SAS (old)IFRS (now)
Balance sheetStatement of Financial Position
Profit and loss accountStatement of Profit or Loss / Statement of Comprehensive Income
Shareholders' fundsEquity
Fixed assetsProperty, Plant and Equipment (PPE)
Notes on accountsNotes to the Financial Statements
Standards to recognise
  • IAS 1: presentation of financial statements
  • IAS 2: inventories, measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value
  • IAS 16: property, plant and equipment, depreciated over useful life
  • IAS 21: foreign currency translation
  • IFRS 15: revenue, five-step model (identify the contract, identify performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it, recognise revenue when each obligation is satisfied)
  • IFRS 9: financial instruments, including expected credit loss impairment, which matters deeply to banks
  • IFRS 16: leases on the balance sheet
Worked example

A company signs a ₦12,000,000 contract to deliver and install equipment. Delivery and installation are separate performance obligations, so the transaction price is allocated between them and revenue is recognised as each is satisfied, not all at signature.

Common trap

IFRS is not a checklist that removes judgement. Estimates, assumptions, policies, and disclosures still matter, and two honest accountants can reach different numbers from the same facts.

Recall prompts
  1. Name the two fundamental qualitative characteristics of useful financial information.
  2. Walk through the IFRS 15 five-step model.
  3. Why does expected credit loss matter to a bank's financial statements?
Show model answers
  1. Relevance and faithful representation.
  2. Identify the contract, identify the performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it, recognise revenue when each obligation is satisfied.
  3. Banks hold large loan books. ECL forces earlier recognition of expected losses, which changes profit, provisions, and capital.
Technology Risk link

Revenue recognition logic, ECL models, and report generation live inside systems. A change to that logic is a change to financial reporting, so controlled change management is not an IT housekeeping task. It is an assurance requirement.

11

Roles and responsibilities of the finance function
Plain-English rule

Finance plans, records, controls, reports, and explains money. Typical responsibilities: record to report, procure to pay, order to cash, treasury and cash management, budgeting and forecasting, tax compliance, financial control, and stakeholder reporting. Internal audit provides independent assurance and does not run the processes it reviews.

Who does what
  • CFO: owns the financial agenda and reports to the board.
  • Financial controller: owns the close, controls, and statutory reporting.
  • Treasurer: owns cash, funding, and banking relationships.
  • Management accountant: owns budgets, forecasts, and performance analysis.
  • Tax lead: owns filings, assessments, and tax risk.
  • Internal audit: independent assurance over the whole system of internal control.
Core responsibilities (from the slides)
  • Accounts payable (AP): capture and validate supplier invoices, route invoices for approval, schedule payments to meet agreed terms, avoid late charges and assess legitimate early-payment discounts, maintain accurate vendor records. Watch the duplicate payment risk.
  • Accounts receivable (AR): prepare and issue customer invoices, track due dates and ageing, follow up overdue balances professionally, allocate receipts to the correct customer or invoice, escalate collection issues. Watch the misallocated receipt risk.
  • Payroll system management: process salaries, wages, allowances and benefits; calculate deductions and contributions (PAYE, pension, NHF and others); ensure statutory remittances are made on time; record payroll expenses and liabilities in the general ledger.
  • Financial reporting: prepare periodic financial statements and management reports; coordinate month-end, quarter-end and year-end close; review results and explain significant movements and variances; ensure information is accurate, complete and timely; support compliance with reporting requirements. Finance does not just report the numbers, it explains what they mean.
  • Cash and treasury management: monitor the daily cash position, prepare and monitor cash flow forecasts, manage bank accounts, ensure funds for operational obligations, manage excess cash and short-term funding, support financing, investment and foreign currency activities. A business can be profitable and still fail on cash management.
  • Tax and regulatory compliance: support the preparation and filing of tax returns, monitor tax and regulatory deadlines, respond to queries from authorities. Some organisations outsource this to external tax consultants.
Interrelation with other departments
  • HR / People: payroll and benefits, headcount and staff cost budgeting, statutory deductions.
  • Sales and marketing: customer invoicing and collections, revenue forecasts, pricing and profitability.
  • Procurement and operations: purchasing and supplier payments, inventory and operating costs, capital expenditure.
  • Tax and legal: tax and regulatory compliance, contractual obligations, legal and compliance matters.
  • IT / technology: finance systems and applications, access controls, data integrity and automation.
  • Senior management: budgets and forecasts, financial performance, investment and strategic decisions.
Worked example

Procure to pay: an approved requisition becomes a purchase order, goods are received with a goods received note, the supplier invoice is matched to both, payment is approved, and the transaction is reconciled. Each stage has an owner and a control.

Common trap

Finance owns many controls, but IT operates the systems, access, interfaces, and change processes those controls depend on. A clean process narrative that ignores the technology layer is an incomplete story.

Recall prompts
  1. Name the core stages of procure to pay.
  2. What does record to report produce, and who owns it?
  3. Where could segregation of duties fail in a payment process?
Show model answers
  1. Requisition, purchase order, goods receipt, invoice, three-way match, approval, payment, reconciliation.
  2. Record to report produces the financial statements and management reports. The financial controller typically owns the close.
  3. When one person can create a supplier, approve the invoice, and release the payment. That enables fictitious supplier fraud.
Technology Risk link

Technology Risk maps business process risk to IT general controls and application controls. Know the process first, then test the technology that supports it. That order is what makes your findings land with finance stakeholders.

12

Key business processes and their controls
Plain-English rule

A business process is a structured set of related activities or tasks performed by people, systems, or departments to achieve a specific business objective and create value for customers or other stakeholders. Processes matter because most business risks arise within them, internal controls are designed around them, financial transactions originate from them, and process knowledge helps you identify inefficiencies, risks, and improvement opportunities.

The five key processes
  • Sales: selling products or services and generating revenue. Activities: developing sales strategies, identifying prospective customers, managing customer relationships, negotiating contracts, processing customer orders, invoicing customers. Risks: sales recorded without delivery, incorrect pricing, revenue leakage, poor customer service leading to lost customers.
  • Procurement: acquiring the right goods, at the right quality, price, supplier, and time. Activities: identifying business needs, requesting quotations, selecting suppliers, issuing purchase orders, receiving goods and services, verifying supplier invoices. Risks: fraudulent purchases, unauthorised suppliers, overpayment, poor-quality materials.
  • Human resources: recruiting, developing, rewarding, and retaining the workforce. Activities: recruitment and selection, onboarding, performance evaluation, training and development, payroll administration and benefits, discipline and exit management. Risks: recruiting unqualified personnel, payroll errors, non-compliance with labour laws, high employee turnover.
  • Finance: managing, recording, monitoring, and reporting financial activities. Activities: recording transactions, budgeting and forecasting, tax management, cash management, financial reporting, receivables and payables management. Risks: inaccurate financial reporting, fraud, cash losses, poor budgeting, regulatory non-compliance.
  • Production: transforming inputs such as raw materials, labour, and machinery into finished goods or services. Activities: production planning, scheduling operations, quality control, managing labour, operating machinery, inventory management. Risks: production delays, equipment breakdown, product defects, excessive waste, inventory shortages.
How it all comes together

HR recruits and manages the employees responsible for the processes. Procurement buys the raw materials. Production converts them into finished goods. Sales sells the finished goods. Finance records the transactions and reports performance. A weakness in one process can affect every other process.

Common trap

A process narrative that only describes the happy path is weak. Risk hides in exceptions, overrides, interfaces, manual workarounds, and month-end pressure. Also do not forget that processes are interconnected: a procurement failure becomes a production failure, which becomes a sales failure.

Recall prompts
  1. Name the five key business processes.
  2. Give two risks in the procurement process.
  3. Why does a weakness in one process affect the others?
Show model answers
  1. Sales, procurement, human resources, finance, and production.
  2. Any two of: fraudulent purchases, unauthorised suppliers, overpayment, poor-quality materials.
  3. They are interconnected: HR staffs them, procurement feeds production, production feeds sales, and finance records everything. An upstream failure shows up downstream.
Technology Risk link

This module is the bridge to IT application controls. Approval thresholds, credit limits, duplicate invoice checks, and three-way matching are all automated controls you will test for design and operating effectiveness.

Regulators in the Nigerian financial system
Plain-English rule

The Nigerian Financial System comprises the institutions, markets, instruments, and regulatory bodies that facilitate the mobilisation and allocation of funds within the economy. It includes banks, insurance companies, pension administrators, stockbrokers and investment firms, capital markets, and other financial institutions. Because these institutions manage public funds and drive economic development, they are subject to oversight by regulatory authorities.

Why regulators matter
  • Promote financial stability.
  • Protect investors and depositors.
  • Maintain confidence in the financial system.
  • Ensure compliance with laws and regulations.
  • Prevent fraud and financial misconduct.
  • Promote transparency and accountability.

Nigerian tax system essentials
Plain-English rule

Tax is a compulsory levy imposed by government on income, consumption, or wealth. Tax has a base (what is taxed), a rate (the percentage), a taxpayer, a filing obligation, an assessment, a payment, and enforcement. Direct taxes hit income and profits. Indirect taxes hit consumption and transactions. Nigeria's tax administration was reshaped by the Nigeria Tax Administration Act 2025, effective January 2026, with the Nigeria Revenue Service taking over from the FIRS.

Understanding tax (from the slides)

A tax is a compulsory financial charge or levy imposed by a government on individuals, businesses, properties, goods, services, or transactions to generate revenue for the provision of public goods and services. Key features: compulsory, legally enforceable, and non-reciprocal. Taxes are not voluntary payments.

Tax reforms in Nigeria, effective 1 January 2026
  • Nigeria Tax Act (NTA) 2025
  • Nigeria Tax Administration Act (NTAA) 2025
  • Nigeria Revenue Service (Establishment) Act (NRSA) 2025
  • Joint Revenue Board (Establishment) Act (JRBA) 2025
  • Petroleum Industry Act (PIA) 2021
  • These replaced or consolidated several previous laws: Companies Income Tax Act, Personal Income Tax Act, Capital Gains Tax Act, VAT Act, Petroleum Profits Tax Act, and Stamp Duties Act.
Three-tier structure of the Nigerian tax system
  • Federal: administers major taxes through the Nigeria Revenue Service (NRS), formerly the Federal Inland Revenue Service (FIRS).
  • State: administers taxes imposed on individuals residing within their jurisdictions, through the various State Internal Revenue Services (SIRS).
  • Local: administers certain local taxes, rates, fees, and levies approved under Nigerian law.
Classification: direct and indirect taxes
  • Direct taxes are imposed directly on the income, profits, gains, or wealth of a taxpayer. The burden cannot generally be transferred. Examples: Companies Income Tax (CIT), Personal Income Tax (PIT), Development Levy, Capital Gains Tax (CGT), Petroleum Profits Tax (PPT), Hydrocarbon Tax (HCT).
  • Indirect taxes are imposed on the consumption of goods and services. The burden can be shifted: a business or intermediary collects the tax and remits it, while the final cost is borne by the consumer. Example: Value Added Tax (VAT).
  • Withholding Tax (WHT) is not a separate tax. It is an advance deduction of tax at source from specified payments.
Takeaways from the slides: memorise these
  1. FIRS is now the Nigeria Revenue Service (NRS).
  2. Small companies (₦100m turnover or less) are exempt from CIT.
  3. TET, NITDA Levy, NASENI Levy and Police Trust Fund Levy have been replaced by a 4% Development Levy.
  4. Individuals earning up to ₦800,000 annually pay no PIT.
  5. CGT for companies increased from 10% to 30%.
  6. PPT still applies to companies with OML or OPL.
  7. HCT applies to companies with PML or PPL.
Worked example

A large company earns ₦200,000,000 of taxable profit. Conceptually, companies income tax at 30% gives ₦60,000,000. The real computation involves capital allowances, disallowed expenses, and minimum tax rules, so treat this as a teaching example, not a filing.

Common trap

Do not memorise stale rates from random notes. The 2026 reforms changed administration, levies, and thresholds. Use the Academy handout for exam detail and the statutes for real work.

Recall prompts
  1. What is the difference between a tax base and a tax rate?
  2. Why is withholding tax described as advance tax?
  3. Why does a tax return need review and approval before filing?
Show model answers
  1. The base is what is taxed (profit, income, consumption). The rate is the percentage applied to that base.
  2. It is deducted at source and offsets the recipient's final liability, so it is tax paid in advance.
  3. Review catches errors and unsupported positions before they attract penalties, interest, and reputational damage. It also documents management oversight.
Technology Risk link

Tax engines and ERP tax configurations create financial reporting risk when master data, rates, or change access are weak. When rates change by statute, the change log becomes audit evidence.

Professional toolkit: evidence, controls, and your first 90 days
Plain-English rule

An audit is a structured argument: risk, control, evidence, conclusion. Working papers must be complete, accurate, clear, and timely. Evidence must be traceable to its source, dated, and reviewable by someone who was not there. If you cannot trace it, you cannot rely on it.

Core vocabulary
  • Test of design: is the control capable of preventing or detecting the risk?
  • Test of operating effectiveness: did it actually work across the period?
  • ITGC: access to programs and data, change management, computer operations, program development.
  • ITAC: automated controls inside applications, such as validations, matches, and thresholds.
  • Population testing: testing 100% of records with analytics instead of a sample.
Worked example

A leaver's account stays active for 45 days after exit. The risk is unauthorised access. The control is timely de-provisioning. The evidence is the HR leaver list joined to the access log. The conclusion is a deficiency, with severity depending on what that account could reach.

Common trap

New analysts lose credibility by overclaiming. "I tested it" without evidence, or a conclusion that outruns the sample, is worse than saying "I need one more piece of evidence". Calibrated language is a professional skill.

Recall prompts
  1. What is the difference between a test of design and a test of operating effectiveness?
  2. Name the four ITGC categories.
  3. Why does 100% population testing beat sampling when the data is reliable?
Show model answers
  1. Design asks whether the control is capable of addressing the risk. Operating effectiveness asks whether it actually worked across the period.
  2. Access to programs and data, change management, computer operations, program development.
  3. It covers every record, exposes outliers and patterns a sample misses, and supports a stronger conclusion when the data is complete and reliable.
Technology Risk link

Your edge as a new analyst is Python and SQL discipline. The plan after the Academy: learn the engagement method first, then propose one approved, safe analytics improvement. Never with client data in personal tools.
