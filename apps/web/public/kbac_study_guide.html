01

Accounting equation and the balance sheet idea
Plain-English rule

Assets are resources the business controls. Liabilities are what it owes to outsiders. Equity is the residual interest of the owners. The equation Assets = Liabilities + Equity must hold after every single transaction.

Expanded version

Assets = Liabilities + Capital + Income − Expenses. Profit increases equity. Drawings and dividends reduce it. This is why the statement of profit or loss connects to the statement of financial position.

Worked example

Owner contributes ₦1,000,000 cash: assets rise ₦1,000,000 and equity rises ₦1,000,000. The business buys equipment for ₦400,000 cash: cash falls ₦400,000 and equipment rises ₦400,000. Assets stay at ₦1,000,000.

Common trap

Profit is not cash. A credit sale raises profit and receivables without a naira entering the bank. A profitable business can still fail from poor cash timing.

Recall prompts
  1. Owner invests cash. Which two elements of the equation move, and in which direction?
  2. A company buys equipment on credit. What happens to assets and liabilities?
  3. Why can a profitable company run out of cash?
Technology Risk link

System interfaces and automated postings must preserve completeness and accuracy. A ledger can look perfectly balanced while a failed interface silently omits part of the population. That is exactly the kind of gap population testing catches.

02

Accounting defined: purpose, users, and types of business
Plain-English rule

Accounting is the process of identifying, recording, classifying, summarising, and communicating economic information about an entity so that users can make informed decisions. It is called the language of business for a reason: it turns activity into numbers people can act on.

Why you learn it
  • To read and interpret financial statements instead of nodding politely at them.
  • To see how business activity becomes reported numbers, and where it can go wrong.
  • To understand what finance teams own, so your Technology Risk questions land in their language.
  • To spot fraud, error, and misstatement risk in systems and processes.
Main types of business
  • Sole proprietorship: one owner, simple to set up, owner has unlimited liability, business is not a separate legal person. Equity shows as a capital account.
  • Partnership: two or more owners sharing capital and profits, generally with unlimited liability in a general partnership. A limited liability partnership limits members' liability.
  • Limited liability company: a separate legal person owned by shareholders whose liability is limited to their shares. Registered under CAMA in Nigeria. Private (Ltd) or public (Plc). Equity shows as share capital plus retained earnings.
Common trap

Do not mix up the owner and the business. In accounting, the business is treated as separate from its owner, which is why drawings and owner contributions are recorded rather than ignored.

Two statements to name in any answer

The balance sheet (statement of financial position) shows assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time. The income statement (profit or loss) shows revenue, expenses, and profit over a period. One is a photograph, the other is a video.

Recall prompts
  1. Define accounting in one sentence without using the word money.
  2. Name the three main types of business and one liability feature of each.
  3. Which statement is a photograph and which is a video?
Technology Risk link

Every business type still produces financial statements, and every financial statement is fed by systems. Your edge is understanding both the business structure and the technology chain behind the numbers.

03

Debits, credits, and the DEALER rule
Plain-English rule

Debit means the left side of an account. Credit means the right side. DEALER: Drawings and dividends, Expenses, Assets increase with a debit. Liabilities, Equity, Revenue increase with a credit. Every transaction has at least one debit and one credit, and they must be equal.

Worked example

Pay ₦80,000 rent in cash: debit Rent Expense ₦80,000, credit Cash ₦80,000. Make a ₦50,000 cash sale: debit Cash ₦50,000, credit Sales Revenue ₦50,000.

Common trap

Debit does not mean good and credit does not mean bad. They are sides of an account, not moral verdicts. Also remember that for a bank, customer deposits are liabilities, because the bank owes that money back.

Recall prompts
  1. Which side increases an asset? Which side increases revenue?
  2. Record a ₦50,000 cash sale without looking.
  3. Why are customer deposits a liability on a bank's balance sheet?
Technology Risk link

Application controls often enforce balanced journal entries at the point of posting. Your audit question is whether the system actually prevents or flags invalid postings, and whether the override path is logged.

04

Double entry and the journal
Plain-English rule

Every transaction touches at least two accounts. The journal records the date, accounts, amounts, narration, and reference. Posting moves those entries into ledger accounts. Double entry is what creates traceability from a source document to the financial statements.

Worked example

Buy inventory on credit for ₦250,000: debit Inventory ₦250,000, credit Accounts Payable ₦250,000. Pay the supplier later: debit Accounts Payable ₦250,000, credit Bank ₦250,000.

Common trap

A balanced entry can still be wrong. It may hit the wrong account, wrong period, wrong entity, or wrong cost centre. Balance proves arithmetic, not truth.

Recall prompts
  1. What is the entry for inventory bought on credit?
  2. Name two ways a balanced journal can still be wrong.
  3. Why is journal approval a control rather than a formality?
Technology Risk link

For an ERP or core banking client, ask who can post, who can approve, what is automated, and whether the audit trail survives. Manual journal entry access is one of the most abused doors in any finance system.

05

T-accounts, normal balances, and closing entries
Plain-English rule

A T-account is the visual form of a ledger account. Debits sit on the left, credits sit on the right. At period end, the difference is the balance: carried down (c/d) on the smaller side and brought down (b/d) on the larger side to start the next period.

Normal balances
  • Debit-balanced accounts: assets, expenses, drawings, dividends, purchases.
  • Credit-balanced accounts: liabilities, equity or capital, revenue or sales, accruals.
  • Contra accounts reverse the pattern: accumulated depreciation is credit-balanced, drawings is debit-balanced.
Closing entries for a sole trader
  1. Close revenue to the profit and loss account: debit sales, credit P&L.
  2. Close expenses and purchases to the P&L account: debit P&L, credit each expense account.
  3. The P&L balance is the profit or loss. Transfer it to capital: profit means debit P&L, credit capital. A loss reverses.
  4. Assets and liabilities are never closed. Their balances are carried forward to the statement of financial position.
Worked mini example

Sales of ₦400,000, purchases of ₦250,000, rent of ₦60,000. Close sales: Dr Sales 400,000 / Cr P&L 400,000. Close purchases and rent: Dr P&L 310,000 / Cr Purchases 250,000 / Cr Rent 60,000. The P&L shows a ₦90,000 profit, transferred to capital: Dr P&L 90,000 / Cr Capital 90,000.

Common trap

Only temporary (nominal) accounts close: revenue, expenses, purchases, and the P&L itself. Permanent (real) accounts such as cash, equipment, loans, and payables stay open and carry forward. Closing a real account destroys the balance sheet.

Recall prompts
  1. Which accounts have a normal debit balance?
  2. Write the four closing steps for a sole trader in order.
  3. Why do asset and liability balances appear on the statement of financial position after closing?
Technology Risk link

Closing and carry-forward are automated in every ERP and core banking system. When period-end routines change in a system upgrade, the closing logic is a financial reporting control that deserves a controlled change record.

06

The accounting cycle and the trial balance
Plain-English rule

The cycle: source documents, journal, post to ledger, extract the trial balance, make adjusting entries, produce the adjusted trial balance, prepare the financial statements, then close the temporary accounts. A trial balance simply lists closing balances and proves total debits equal total credits.

Worked example

Total debits of ₦4,200,000 against total credits of ₦4,200,000: the trial balance agrees. It still tells you nothing about whether a whole transaction was omitted or an amount was posted to the wrong account.

Common trap

A balanced trial balance is not proof of accuracy. Errors of omission (no entry at all), errors of commission (wrong account), and compensating errors all survive a balanced trial balance.

Recall prompts
  1. List the steps of the accounting cycle in order.
  2. What exactly does a trial balance prove, and what does it not prove?
  3. Where do adjusting entries fit in the cycle?
Technology Risk link

Automated trial balances depend on interfaces, posting rules, and period controls. When a system upgrade changes posting logic, the trial balance can still balance while the meaning of a balance changes. That is why change management is a financial reporting control.

07

Accruals, prepayments, depreciation, and other adjustments
Plain-English rule

The accrual basis matches income and expenses to the period they belong to, not the period cash moves. Accruals record expenses or income before cash moves. Prepayments record cash before the benefit is consumed. Depreciation allocates the cost of a long-lived asset across its useful life.

Worked examples
  • Unpaid December rent of ₦100,000: debit Rent Expense, credit Accrued Expenses.
  • Prepaid insurance of ₦120,000 for twelve months: debit Prepaid Insurance, credit Cash, then release ₦10,000 monthly to expense.
  • Equipment of ₦1,200,000 over five years, straight line: ₦240,000 depreciation each year. Debit Depreciation Expense, credit Accumulated Depreciation.
  • Customer owes ₦30,000 and will not pay: debit Impairment Expense, credit Trade Receivables.
Common trap

Depreciation is not a cash payment. It is the allocation of a past cash cost. Accumulated depreciation is a contra-asset account and grows over the asset's life, while the asset stays at cost in the ledger.

Recall prompts
  1. Give the two entries for an accrued expense and for a prepayment.
  2. Compute straight-line depreciation on ₦1,200,000 over five years with no residual value.
  3. Why is accumulated depreciation not deducted from the asset account itself?
Technology Risk link

Adjustment calculations are often automated, which makes the underlying configuration, master data, and change history audit-relevant. If depreciation logic changes after a system patch, someone must have approved and tested that change.

08

The complete set of financial statements
Plain-English rule

IAS 1 requires a complete set: (1) statement of financial position at the reporting date, (2) statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income for the period, (3) statement of changes in equity, (4) statement of cash flows, (5) notes including accounting policies, plus comparative figures for the prior period.

What each one answers
  • Financial position: what do we own and owe right now?
  • Profit or loss: how did we perform over the period?
  • Changes in equity: what moved owners' interest and why?
  • Cash flows: where did cash actually come from and go?
  • Notes: the detail and judgement behind the numbers.
Worked example

Classify: customer receipts are operating cash flow. Buying equipment is investing. Repaying a bank loan is financing. Depreciation appears in profit or loss but never as a cash payment, so it is adjusted for in the operating section of the cash flow statement.

Common trap

The statement of financial position is a photograph at one date. The statement of profit or loss is a video of the whole period. Mixing those two ideas is the fastest way to lose easy marks.

Recall prompts
  1. Name the five components of a complete set of financial statements.
  2. Classify loan repayment, equipment purchase, and customer receipts into cash flow categories.
  3. Why are the notes not optional?
Technology Risk link

Learn which applications, interfaces, and end-of-day jobs feed each statement. Financial statement assurance is often technology assurance wearing a different jacket.

09

Financial statement analysis and ratios
Plain-English rule

Analysis turns numbers into meaning. Profitability ratios measure earning power. Liquidity ratios measure the ability to meet short-term obligations. Leverage ratios measure debt exposure. Efficiency ratios measure how well working capital is managed. Always compare across time and against the industry.

Core formulas
  • Gross margin = gross profit ÷ revenue
  • Net margin = net profit ÷ revenue
  • Return on equity = net profit ÷ total equity
  • Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities
  • Quick ratio = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities
  • Debt to equity = total debt ÷ total equity
  • Interest cover = earnings before interest and tax ÷ interest expense
  • Receivable days = receivables ÷ revenue × 365
Worked example

Current assets ₦8,000,000, inventory ₦2,000,000, current liabilities ₦4,000,000. Current ratio is 2.0. Quick ratio is (8,000,000 − 2,000,000) ÷ 4,000,000 = 1.5. The business can cover short-term debts without selling inventory.

Common trap

A high current ratio can hide slow-moving inventory or receivables that will never be collected. Ratios are questions, not conclusions. High profit with weak operating cash flow is a classic quality-of-earnings warning.

Recall prompts
  1. Write the current ratio and quick ratio formulas.
  2. A company has strong profit but weak operating cash flow. Name two possible causes.
  3. Why does ROE matter more to owners than net margin alone?
Technology Risk link

Analytics work depends on clean data. Duplicate customers, orphaned invoices, and stale master data quietly distort every ratio. Population-level testing exists precisely because samples hide these patterns.

10

IFRS: the framework and the standards worth knowing
Plain-English rule

IFRS is a principles-based set of standards that gives global users a common basis for general-purpose financial statements. The Conceptual Framework sets the objective (useful information for decisions) and the fundamental qualitative characteristics: relevance and faithful representation. Enhancing characteristics are comparability, verifiability, timeliness, and understandability.

Standards to recognise
  • IAS 1: presentation of financial statements
  • IAS 2: inventories, measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value
  • IAS 16: property, plant and equipment, depreciated over useful life
  • IAS 21: foreign currency translation
  • IFRS 15: revenue, five-step model (identify the contract, identify performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it, recognise revenue when each obligation is satisfied)
  • IFRS 9: financial instruments, including expected credit loss impairment, which matters deeply to banks
  • IFRS 16: leases on the balance sheet
Worked example

A company signs a ₦12,000,000 contract to deliver and install equipment. Delivery and installation are separate performance obligations, so the transaction price is allocated between them and revenue is recognised as each is satisfied, not all at signature.

Common trap

IFRS is not a checklist that removes judgement. Estimates, assumptions, policies, and disclosures still matter, and two honest accountants can reach different numbers from the same facts.

Recall prompts
  1. Name the two fundamental qualitative characteristics of useful financial information.
  2. Walk through the IFRS 15 five-step model.
  3. Why does expected credit loss matter to a bank's financial statements?
Technology Risk link

Revenue recognition logic, ECL models, and report generation live inside systems. A change to that logic is a change to financial reporting, so controlled change management is not an IT housekeeping task. It is an assurance requirement.

11

Roles and responsibilities of the finance function
Plain-English rule

Finance plans, records, controls, reports, and explains money. Typical responsibilities: record to report, procure to pay, order to cash, treasury and cash management, budgeting and forecasting, tax compliance, financial control, and stakeholder reporting. Internal audit provides independent assurance and does not run the processes it reviews.

Who does what
  • CFO: owns the financial agenda and reports to the board.
  • Financial controller: owns the close, controls, and statutory reporting.
  • Treasurer: owns cash, funding, and banking relationships.
  • Management accountant: owns budgets, forecasts, and performance analysis.
  • Tax lead: owns filings, assessments, and tax risk.
  • Internal audit: independent assurance over the whole system of internal control.
Core responsibilities from the Academy session
  • Accounts payable management: supplier invoices, three-way matching, payment runs, reconciliations, and managing the payables ledger.
  • Accounts receivable management: customer invoicing, credit control, collections, and managing the receivables ledger.
  • Payroll system management: processing salaries, statutory deductions (PAYE, pension, NHF), and controls over changes to payroll data.
  • Financial reporting: preparing financial statements under IFRS, with audit work performed under ISA (International Standards on Auditing).
  • Cash and treasury management: cash positioning, bank relationships, funding, and liquidity.
  • Tax and regulatory compliance: filings, remittances, and staying current with the regulators.
Worked example

Procure to pay: an approved requisition becomes a purchase order, goods are received with a goods received note, the supplier invoice is matched to both, payment is approved, and the transaction is reconciled. Each stage has an owner and a control.

Common trap

Finance owns many controls, but IT operates the systems, access, interfaces, and change processes those controls depend on. A clean process narrative that ignores the technology layer is an incomplete story.

Recall prompts
  1. Name the core stages of procure to pay.
  2. What does record to report produce, and who owns it?
  3. Where could segregation of duties fail in a payment process?
Technology Risk link

Technology Risk maps business process risk to IT general controls and application controls. Know the process first, then test the technology that supports it. That order is what makes your findings land with finance stakeholders.

12

Key business processes and their controls
Plain-English rule

A process converts inputs into controlled outputs. For any process, learn the trigger, the people, the systems, the approvals, the records, the exceptions, the reconciliations, and the reporting. Controls are either preventive (stop it happening) or detective (find it after), and either manual or automated.

The three big flows
  • Procure to pay: requisition, purchase order, goods receipt, invoice, three-way match, approval, payment, reconciliation.
  • Order to cash: customer order, credit check, fulfilment, invoice, collection, cash application, reconciliation.
  • Record to report: close calendar, journal processing, reconciliations, consolidation, financial reporting.
Worked example

The three-way match compares purchase order, goods received note, and supplier invoice. Quantity and price must agree before payment. It is a preventive automated control, and it is one of the most testable IT application controls in any ERP.

Common trap

A process narrative that only describes the happy path is weak. Risk hides in exceptions, overrides, interfaces, manual workarounds, and month-end pressure.

Recall prompts
  1. What three documents does a three-way match compare?
  2. Give one preventive and one detective control in order to cash.
  3. Why do reconciliations matter even when systems are automated?
Technology Risk link

This module is the bridge to IT application controls. Approval thresholds, credit limits, duplicate invoice checks, and three-way matching are all automated controls you will test for design and operating effectiveness.

14

Nigerian tax system essentials
Plain-English rule

Tax is a compulsory levy imposed by government on income, consumption, or wealth. Tax has a base (what is taxed), a rate (the percentage), a taxpayer, a filing obligation, an assessment, a payment, and enforcement. Direct taxes hit income and profits. Indirect taxes hit consumption and transactions. Nigeria's tax administration was reshaped by the Nigeria Tax Administration Act 2025, effective January 2026, with the Nigeria Revenue Service taking over from the FIRS.

Overview of taxation: what it is for
  • Revenue: funding public goods, infrastructure, security, and services.
  • Redistribution: moving resources toward social objectives and equity.
  • Regulation: discouraging or encouraging behaviour through tax.
  • Protection: tariffs and duties can shield local industry.
The Nigerian tax environment
  • Three tiers of government, each with taxing powers: federal, state, and local government.
  • Many taxes and levies, which is why harmonisation and multiple-taxation complaints are constant themes.
  • A large informal sector, which makes administration and compliance a permanent challenge.
  • Reform direction under the Nigeria Tax Act 2025 and the Nigeria Tax Administration Act 2025: simplified administration, consolidated levies, and a stronger revenue service.
  • A Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN) is the anchor of compliance, with e-filing and self-assessment now standard.
Administration of taxation in Nigeria
  • Federal: the Nigeria Revenue Service (NRS) administers federal taxes, succeeding the FIRS.
  • State: State Internal Revenue Services handle personal income tax and state levies. Lagos runs LIRS.
  • FCT: the FCT Internal Revenue Service handles the capital territory.
  • Local government: local revenue committees handle minor levies within their limits.
  • Assessment can be self-assessed or raised by the authority, followed by payment, enforcement, and objection or appeal rights.
Classification of taxes
  • Direct versus indirect: direct taxes are borne by the taxpayer (companies income tax, personal income tax, capital gains tax). Indirect taxes are collected by intermediaries and passed on (VAT, customs duties, excise).
  • By base: income taxes, consumption taxes, and wealth or property taxes.
  • By rate structure: progressive (rate rises with the base), proportional (flat rate), and regressive (falls hardest on lower income earners).
Taxes to recognise
  • Companies income tax on company profits, with a 30% rate for large companies and exemptions for qualifying small companies.
  • VAT at 7.5% on most goods and services.
  • Withholding tax deducted at source on many payments, functioning as advance tax.
  • Personal income tax, progressive, with reliefs including rent relief under the new framework.
  • Development levy consolidated under the new tax acts, replacing several older levies.
  • Capital gains tax and stamp duties on qualifying transactions.
Worked example

A large company earns ₦200,000,000 of taxable profit. Conceptually, companies income tax at 30% gives ₦60,000,000. The real computation involves capital allowances, disallowed expenses, and minimum tax rules, so treat this as a teaching example, not a filing.

Common trap

Do not memorise stale rates from random notes. The 2026 reforms changed administration, levies, and thresholds. Use the Academy handout for exam detail and the statutes for real work.

Recall prompts
  1. What is the difference between a tax base and a tax rate?
  2. Why is withholding tax described as advance tax?
  3. Why does a tax return need review and approval before filing?
Technology Risk link

Tax engines and ERP tax configurations create financial reporting risk when master data, rates, or change access are weak. When rates change by statute, the change log becomes audit evidence.

16

Professional toolkit: evidence, controls, and your first 90 days
Plain-English rule

An audit is a structured argument: risk, control, evidence, conclusion. Working papers must be complete, accurate, clear, and timely. Evidence must be traceable to its source, dated, and reviewable by someone who was not there. If you cannot trace it, you cannot rely on it.

Core vocabulary
  • Test of design: is the control capable of preventing or detecting the risk?
  • Test of operating effectiveness: did it actually work across the period?
  • ITGC: access to programs and data, change management, computer operations, program development.
  • ITAC: automated controls inside applications, such as validations, matches, and thresholds.
  • Population testing: testing 100% of records with analytics instead of a sample.
Worked example

A leaver's account stays active for 45 days after exit. The risk is unauthorised access. The control is timely de-provisioning. The evidence is the HR leaver list joined to the access log. The conclusion is a deficiency, with severity depending on what that account could reach.

Common trap

New analysts lose credibility by overclaiming. "I tested it" without evidence, or a conclusion that outruns the sample, is worse than saying "I need one more piece of evidence". Calibrated language is a professional skill.

Recall prompts
  1. What is the difference between a test of design and a test of operating effectiveness?
  2. Name the four ITGC categories.
  3. Why does 100% population testing beat sampling when the data is reliable?
Technology Risk link

Your edge as a new analyst is Python and SQL discipline. The plan after the Academy: learn the engagement method first, then propose one approved, safe analytics improvement. Never with client data in personal tools.
